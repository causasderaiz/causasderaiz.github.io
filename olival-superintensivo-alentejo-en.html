No. 155 ◐ Monitoring Biodiversity and agro-industrial model

Super-intensive olive groves: environmental groups and Alentejo villages challenge the agro-industrial model straining water, soil and birds

Alentejo, Alqueva irrigation area and other hedgerow groves

◐ 74,059 ha of olives irrigated by Alqueva in 2024 · night harvesting sanctioned by the ICNF since 2020

Irrigation from Alqueva brought hedgerow olive groves, with up to 2,500 trees per hectare, in place of dryland farming, montado and streams. By 2024 they covered 74,059 irrigated hectares. Quercus, SPEA, ZERO and the Alentejo Vivo movement denounce the water use, aquifer salinisation, erosion, pesticides sprayed next to homes, eutrophication of reservoirs, felling of holm oaks, loss of birds and precarious work. Night harvesting, which sucks birds out of the hedgerows, is one of these practices.

What is at stake

Hedgerow olive groves are a drip-irrigated monoculture, planted in continuous rows and harvested by machines that straddle the trees. Over 80% of Alqueva's water goes to intensive olive and almond groves, and permanent crops covered 83% of the irrigated area in 2024. Between 2007 and 2020 the sector received over a billion euros in farm subsidies, and most of the irrigated land is held by a few companies. Dryland cereal, habitat of the little bustard, and holm oak montado give way to hedgerows which, by EDIA's own account, hold half the species of traditional groves. ZERO calls this transformation of the landscape an ecocide.

The water. The Divor and Maranhão reservoirs supply groves of hundreds to thousands of hectares; at Maranhão, Quercus linked cyanobacteria to the olives on its shores. The Moura-Ficalho aquifer is at risk of salinisation.

Soil and montado. Deep ploughing, erosion and agrochemicals contaminate the soil. At Herdade da Ínsua, Serpa, permission was sought to fell 613 holm oaks, many centuries old; Quercus asked for a 25-year ban on intensive olives there.

The birds. Night harvesting kills 17 species, mostly migratory and protected, among them song thrush, goldfinch, greenfinch, chaffinch, serin, siskin, Sardinian warbler and Iberian chiffchaff. The ICNF counted 6.4 dead birds per hectare in Avis; Quercus estimates 70,000 to 100,000 per harvest. The little bustard has lost 90% of its population in twenty years.

The villages. In Ervidel, Nossa Senhora das Neves and Veiros, groves were planted 10 to 20 metres from homes, with pesticides sprayed without warning. Quercus asks for a 200-metre buffer. In Fortes, residents have breathed fumes from a pomace factory for over ten years.

The workers. Mechanised harvesting and pruning rely on seasonal, poorly paid and often migrant labour. ZERO and the Alentejo Vivo movement denounce the exploitation of these workers.
