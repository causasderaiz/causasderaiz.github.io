No. 68 ◐ Monitoring Land-use planning

Arrábida Natural Park: quarries, concrete and tourism close in on the most protected land near Lisbon

Serra da Arrábida: Sesimbra, Setúbal and Palmela

◐ Simultaneous pressure on several fronts, with no updated plan since the park was created

Created in 1976, Arrábida Natural Park protects 12,328 hectares and forms part of the Arrábida/Espichel Special Conservation Area (Natura 2000), with 32 protected habitat types. Yet no instrument today assesses the cumulative pressure surrounding it: 11 quarries already occupy 323 hectares inside the park (more than double the area in 1976, only two of which are being rehabilitated), including Secil's, which runs one of the country's largest cement plants there; a court case disputes public ownership of five beaches in Setúbal ("Hands Off Arrábida!"); the Forte Velho do Outão fort was granted as a 50-year concession to a hotel group; and four tourism megaprojects in Meco and Azóia, with over 4,000 planned beds, are moving forward without a cumulative Environmental Impact Assessment, despite sharing the same protected area, the reason behind the Meco Livre Movement's "SOS Meco" petition. The Frade cave spider, Europe's smallest and critically endangered, survives in a single cave in the range threatened by these same quarries. According to the association Arrábida - Natureza e Comunidade, the built-up area inside the park has grown by around 500% since the 1960s.

Arrábida Natural Park was created in 1976, after decades of mobilisation by naturalists and local residents; the seed was planted when poet Sebastião da Gama, back in the 1940s, raised the alarm over tree-felling in the Mata do Solitário. Today it protects 12,328 hectares of limestone hills by the sea, and forms part of the Arrábida/Espichel Special Conservation Area (Natura 2000), with 32 habitat types identified as conservation priorities.

Despite that status, no plan today assesses the combined effect of several pressures advancing at the same time. There are 11 quarries within the park's boundaries, occupying 323 hectares (more than double the area they occupied in 1976), of which only two are undergoing environmental rehabilitation. Among them is Secil's operation, which runs one of the country's largest cement plants there; the Arrábida Natural Park Management Plan (POPNA) explicitly bans new quarries or the expansion of existing ones, but expansion requests have already reached public consultation, with formal opposition from associations such as ZERO and LPN.

History of the opposition to Secil's quarries: seven environmental organisations, including LPN, formally demanded that the company present a decommissioning and landscape recovery plan for the areas already mined, with a defined timeline; to date, Secil has not publicly responded with a concrete timeline and mining continues within the protected area. A request to expand the quarry was later taken to public consultation, but was ultimately rejected by the regional development commission (CCDR).

Urban pressure compounds the mining pressure. The association Arrábida - Natureza e Comunidade estimates that the built-up area inside the park has grown by around 500% since the 1960s. In Setúbal, the company Palácio da Comenda is disputing in court the public ownership of five beaches (Rasca, Comenda, Rainha, Esguelha and Albarquel), contested by the "Hands Off Arrábida!" movement through a petition delivered to Parliament. The Forte Velho do Outão, a former coastal defence battery inside the park, was handed over as a 50-year concession to a restaurant group for a 5-star hotel.

In Meco and Azóia, on the Sesimbra side, four tourism megaprojects plan more than 3,000 new tourist beds, all within the same Arrábida/Espichel protected area, without a cumulative Environmental Impact Assessment covering all four projects together; the Meco Livre Movement has led that fight since 2021, with petitions delivered to Parliament. For the full chronology of this struggle, see the Meco Livre case.

The most threatened species across this whole territory may be the Frade cave spider, Europe's smallest, Critically Endangered: it survives in a single cave in the Arrábida range, precisely in an area surrounded by quarries. A public petition calls for formal protection of its habitat.

None of these processes is assessed together. It is that absence of a territory-wide view (the same mountain range, the same aquifers, the same protected area) that makes Arrábida one of the most complete cases of cumulative pressure on a protected area in Portugal.

What you can do now

  • Monitor. When a public consultation or deadline opens, that is when participation has legal effect.
  • Share the sources, not just the headline. They are all listed above.
  • Talk to those already on the ground: LPN, ZERO, Quercus, Arrábida - Natureza e Comunidade, Hands Off Arrábida Movement, Meco Livre Movement.
  • If an infraction is happening, know who to report to before calling.
